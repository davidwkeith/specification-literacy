# Problem Architecture for Working Professionals: Specifying, Verifying, and Directing AI at Work

> **Working title — provisional.** The alternative under consideration is *"Specification Literacy: Telling AI What You Actually Mean."* Either way, the registrar copy will lead with the second clause: this is a course about getting AI tools to do what you actually meant, and about knowing — before the consequences land — whether they did.

This document is the **course outline** for a continuing-education class that any community college can offer. It is the adult parallel to the 8th-grade anchor course *Introduction to Problem Architecture: Specification Literacy Through Algorithms and AI*, described in [`../index.md`](../index.md) §10.3 and documented separately there exactly as this outline is documented separately here. It is the **formal vehicle for "Band 5: Adult Learners — Specification Under Stakes"** ([`../index.md`](../index.md) §10.5): the point in the developmental progression where the interpreter — an AI, a colleague, a vendor — acts on the learner's behalf with autonomy, and the cost of underspecification lands immediately on the learner rather than on a returned assignment. The cohort is a mixed-field group of working professionals — analysts, project managers, paralegals, marketers, engineers, operations staff, clinicians, anyone who already writes specifications to an AI system whether or not they call it that. The pilot and evaluation design for this cohort — recruitment, informed consent, success criteria, iteration cadence, and the hand-off to the bands below — is documented in the companion file [`pilot-plan.md`](./pilot-plan.md).

The course inherits the framework's central norm, the **Verification Principle** ([`../index.md`](../index.md) §4): *the output is the beginning, not the end.* For an adult at work that reads: when you hand a brief, a ticket, a prompt, or a contract to something that will act on it, your job is to check whether the result matches what you meant — and to figure out why it didn't. The four behaviors that constitute that job, and that this course is built to develop, are the framework's four: **specify** what a correct result would look like before anything is generated; **evaluate** what comes back against that specification rather than against "looks reasonable"; **explain** why a correct result is correct, not merely observe that it is; and **identify the limits** — the preconditions, the cases not handled, the tradeoffs baked in.

---

## 1. Format

**Eight weekly evening sessions of three hours each = 24 contact hours.** Eight weeks, one evening a week. Each session is followed by a between-session **field assignment**: apply that week's idea at your actual job, on a real artifact, and bring the artifact — and what happened when you ran it — back to the next session. The field assignments are the spine of the course. The classroom is where the method is named, modeled, and practiced on shared examples; the workplace is where it is exercised under stakes; the next session is where the workplace result is debriefed. Nothing here is hypothetical for long.

**Alternative format (same 24 hours, different shape):** a two-day weekend intensive — Saturday and Sunday, roughly six hours each, covering Sessions 1–4 of the arc below — followed by four follow-up weekday evenings of three hours each, covering Sessions 5–8 at one-per-week, so the field-assignment loop still gets four iterations. This shape suits an employer-sponsored cohort that can release people for two consecutive days but not for eight consecutive weeks. The weekly evening format is the default; the intensive is offered when a sponsoring organization asks for it.

A standing housekeeping note for either format: participants are on laptops, and the room has whiteboards. The instructor's standing instruction to the room is "bring the real thing, redacted" — see §6 and §7.

## 2. The medium, and why it differs from the 8th-grade course

The 8th-grade anchor course teaches specification literacy through **five sorting algorithms**. It does that for the reasons set out in [`../index.md`](../index.md) §3: an algorithmic problem gives **immediate, unambiguous feedback** (the generated code meets the spec or it doesn't, in seconds); it permits **formal verification** (a 13-year-old can trace a binary search on paper and *prove* it correct, not merely watch it work); and, with an AI doing the coding, it makes **underspecification visible** — the student writes what feels like a clear description, hands it to the AI, and discovers the AI resolved an ambiguity the student never saw. Algorithms are the ideal *pedagogical* medium because they offer all three at once.

Adults at work are not all coders, and most of their specification work — the prompts, briefs, tickets, PRDs, RFPs, contract clauses, delegation emails — lives in a domain where the third property (AI makes underspecification visible) is available but the first two are not. You can hand a brief to an AI and watch it diverge from what you meant — that loop closes in minutes — but you generally *cannot prove* an AI-drafted board summary, market analysis, or project plan correct. You can only spot-check it. So the medium of this course cannot be "five sorting algorithms," and it cannot be "five anything algorithmic," because that would be a course for the engineers in the room and a spectator sport for everyone else.

The medium is therefore a mix:

- **Roughly 70%: real workplace specification artifacts, at increasing stakes.** The arc moves from low-stakes, fast-feedback artifacts to high-stakes, slow-feedback ones — **prompts → briefs → tickets / PRDs → contracts / SOWs** — and the artifacts are the participants' own, brought from their actual jobs. This is where most of the course lives, and it is deliberately the domain where verification is *merely plausible*: where the honest answer to "is this right?" is "it looks reasonable, and I spot-checked the parts I could." Most professional verification is exactly this, and naming it as such — distinguishing "looks reasonable" from "meets the spec" — is half the point.

- **Roughly 30%: one running read-and-trace code thread.** A single short program — in Python, the same language as the 8th-grade anchor course, chosen for its readability, not because anyone in the room writes it — traced and verified by hand across Sessions 4 and 5, the one place in the course where verification is *provable*, where a participant can put a finger on the page and demonstrate that the thing must be correct (or find the bug). Per [`../index.md`](../index.md) §6.1: participants **read and trace** code; they **do not write** it. The AI writes the code; participants verify it. This thread exists so that every adult in the room — including the ones who have never written a line of code — tastes provable verification *at least once*, and feels the contrast with the merely-plausible verification that dominates the other 70% of the course and the whole of their professional lives. The contrast is the lesson: a few domains let you prove; most don't; knowing which kind of domain you're in changes what "I checked it" is allowed to mean.

The code thread **adapts the structure of the high-school AP CS A capstone** [`ap-cs-a-capstone`](https://github.com/davidwkeith/ap-cs-a-capstone) ("The Java Architect Capstone"), in which students architect a text-adventure game as a written specification, hand it to a custom AI builder, and then must be able to **explain every line** at a walkthrough — "the AI wrote it" scores the lowest band, and the teams that rush the blueprint get broken games. The adult version is **scaled down to read-and-verify**: adults do not architect the program and do not write it; they receive an AI-written program built from a stated spec, and their job is to trace it, verify it against that spec, find any gap, and explain why the correct version is correct. The capstone's "you must be able to explain every line" becomes the course's "you must be able to explain why this is correct, line by line, before you sign off."

## 3. Session-by-session arc

Eight sessions. For each: an **in-class focus** (what happens in the room, with concrete activities) and a **field assignment** (what participants do at work before the next session, and bring back). The arc is fixed; the in-class activities below are worked examples of how to fill three hours, not the only way to fill them.

### Session 1 — The gap

**In-class focus.** The course opens with the **Adult Sandwich Challenge**. The 8th-grade course's "draw a house" / "make a sandwich" exercise — write an instruction, watch an interpreter follow it literally, see the gap — is moved to the desk of a working professional. Everyone in the room writes a real prompt, or a real delegation email, for a real task they actually have right now ("draft the Q3 update for the board," "summarize this RFP response," "ask the contractor to fix the drainage"). They run it — against the AI tool they brought, or, if the task is a human delegation, by walking through what they'd actually send. Then the group examines, artifact by artifact, the **divergence between what each person meant and what came back**: the board summary that foregrounded the wrong number, the RFP summary that dropped the disqualifying clause, the contractor email that left "fix the drainage" to mean three different jobs at three different prices. We name the phenomenon — **underspecification** ([`../index.md`](../index.md) §2): a description that felt complete to its author because they unconsciously relied on context, defaults, or assumptions the interpreter didn't share. The critical word, on the whiteboard all evening, is *unconsciously*: nobody in the room thought they were being vague. We close by introducing the **Verification Principle** ([`../index.md`](../index.md) §4) and its four behaviors — specify, evaluate, explain, identify limits — as the frame for the next seven weeks, and we introduce the **verification journal** (§4 below) as the place every field assignment gets recorded.

**Field assignment.** Bring back a real prompt, brief, ticket, or delegation email from your work that *didn't quite work* — something where the result was off, the back-and-forth was longer than it should have been, or the thing you got wasn't the thing you needed. Bring the artifact, the result, and a sentence or two on what went wrong. Redact anything confidential (see §7). First verification-journal entry.

### Session 2 — The Assumption Audit, adult version

**In-class focus.** This is the 8th-grade course's central diagnostic — the **Assumption Audit**, the systematic search for hidden assumptions ([`../index.md`](../index.md) §10.3) — re-aimed at workplace specifications. The audit asks four questions of any artifact:

- **Types** — *what kind of thing did I actually ask for?* "Summarize this" — a paragraph? a one-pager? bullets? a slide? "Pull our top customers" — a list? a chart? a ranked table with revenue attached? The interpreter has to pick a type; if you didn't state one, you delegated the choice.
- **Boundaries** — *how much, by when, for whom?* Length, scope, deadline, audience, level of detail. "For the board" is a boundary; "make it short" is not.
- **Defaults** — *what did I leave the interpreter to pick?* Every place the spec is silent is a place something gets decided without you. The audit makes the silences explicit.
- **Context** — *what do I know that I didn't say?* This is the **expert's curse** ([`../index.md`](../index.md) §10.5): the more you know about your field, the more has become automatic, the more you leave unsaid, and the more invisible the omission is to you. "Obviously the Q3 numbers" — obvious to whom? which Q3? on which basis? The expertise *is* the underspecification.

We run the audit **live, on the artifacts participants brought back from Session 1**. The instructor's job here is relentless: surface the buried assumption every time ("you said 'the usual format' — say what the usual format is, out loud, now"), because the participant cannot see it — that's what makes it buried. Each participant leaves with their Session-1 artifact annotated against all four questions.

**Field assignment.** Run the four-question Assumption Audit on a *second* artifact from your work — ideally one you're about to send, not one already sent — and write down what you found under each heading. Journal entry.

### Session 3 — Specifying to an AI that acts

**In-class focus.** Now we move from diagnosing bad specs to writing good ones. Three distinctions anchor the session. First, **a prompt is a specification** — when you prompt an LLM you are writing a spec, and the better it states types, boundaries, defaults, and context, the less the model has to guess. Second, **a system prompt or standing instruction is a *standing* spec** — the persistent context, the custom instructions, the "always do it this way" you set once and the tool applies to everything: that's a spec too, and it's the one most professionals never audit. Third, and most important: **the difference between asking for *output* and asking for *a thing that meets stated criteria*.** "Write the board summary" asks for output — whatever comes back is, by construction, "the output," so there's nothing to check it against. "Write a board summary that (a) leads with the revenue variance, (b) fits on one page, (c) names the three risks from the risk register, (d) ends with the one decision we need from the board" asks for a thing that meets criteria — and now you can check it. The criteria are **acceptance criteria**, and in the room we practice converting brought artifacts from the first kind of request into the second. Whiteboard exercise: take three participants' Session-2 audits and turn each one into a spec with an explicit acceptance-criteria list.

**Field assignment.** Take the artifact you've been working with — or a fresh one — and **rewrite it as a spec with explicit success criteria** (acceptance criteria you could check against). Run it (against your AI tool, or as a human delegation). Then **journal the gap**: what did you ask for, what did you expect, what did you get, where did the interpreter decide something you'd left undecided even after the rewrite? Journal entry, in the full template (§4).

### Session 4 — Verification I: checking plausible work

**In-class focus.** This session is about the verification you do in the 70% domain — where you can't prove, only check. We take an **AI-generated document, analysis, or plan** (some brought by participants, one provided by the instructor for shared work) and evaluate it **against its spec** — line by line where possible, claim by claim, criterion by criterion. The discipline being built is the distinction the whole course turns on: **"looks reasonable" vs. "meets the spec."** A plausible-looking analysis can be wrong; a spec-meeting analysis has been checked against something. We practice **red-teaming your own spec**: read your spec the way an indifferent interpreter would, and find the *two readings* — the place where your words admit an interpretation you didn't intend. (This is the same move the framework calls "find the ambiguities" — [`../index.md`](../index.md) §5; it is also exactly what the AP CS A capstone calls classifying a bug as a "spec ambiguity" rather than a "spec gap.") Then, in the last hour, we **introduce the code thread**: the instructor hands out a short AI-written function — a few dozen lines, with the one-paragraph spec it was built from — and the room **traces it**: reads it line by line, follows the values through, and answers "does this do what the spec says?" Nobody writes code. The function is read.

**Field assignment.** Take an AI-generated work product you actually received this week — a draft, an analysis, a summary, a plan — and evaluate it against the spec you gave (or, if you gave no real spec, reconstruct the spec it *should* have met, then evaluate against that). Note every place "looks reasonable" was doing the work that "meets the spec" should have done. Journal entry.

### Session 5 — Verification II: when proof is possible

**In-class focus.** The code thread continues, and this is the session that delivers the *one taste of provable verification* the medium (§2) is built around. The room **traces a sorting routine or a search by hand** — same posture as the 8th-grade anchor course, scaled to read-only — and then *proves it correct*, or finds the bug, the way [`../index.md`](../index.md) §3.2 describes: a binary search must find the target if it's in the sorted list, and must correctly report "not found" if it isn't, and you can demonstrate *why* it must, with your finger on the page. This is a genuine proof, and most people in the room have never been walked through one. Then the contrast: hold this next to the **Session-4 document case**, where you could only ever spot-check. Why does this domain let you prove and that one doesn't? (Because the algorithm's behavior is fully determined by its spec and a finite argument covers all cases; the board summary's "correctness" is stakeholder-dependent and unbounded — [`../index.md`](../index.md) §3.2, §10.5.) And the operative question for the rest of your working life: **most of your work is the "most" case** — the un-provable kind — so what do you do there? The answer the course gives: state acceptance criteria you *can* check; spot-check the rest deliberately and on the record rather than by vibe; and never let "I read it and it looked fine" stand in for verification you didn't do. Knowing which kind of domain you're in is itself a verification skill.

**Field assignment.** No new code — instead, find the one place this week where you signed off on something (yours or someone else's, AI-generated or not) on the strength of "it looked fine," and write down what an actual check would have looked like, and whether the domain even permitted a proof. Journal entry.

### Session 6 — Specifying across people and organizations

**In-class focus.** Specification leaves your desk and crosses organizational lines: **delegation, RFPs, statements of work, handoffs, tickets and PRDs, "reasonable efforts" clauses.** This is [`../index.md`](../index.md) §10.4's *legal-studies* content — contract drafting as specification — lived rather than simulated: **defined terms are type declarations** ("'Deliverable' means…"), **conditions precedent are preconditions** ("subject to receipt of…"), indemnification clauses are error handling, and **"reasonable efforts" is the professional world's "room temperature"** — it feels obvious to the person who wrote it and resolves differently in the hands of whoever has to act on it, exactly the underspecification mechanism of [`../index.md`](../index.md) §2, now with money attached. And it is also [`../index.md`](../index.md) §10.5's *Management* point: **delegating to a person is writing a specification** — the manager surprised by what an employee delivered has an underspecification problem, not a bad employee; the instruction relied on context the manager never made explicit. In the room we run the Assumption Audit on a real contract clause and a real work handoff, and we draft a one-paragraph SOW scope statement with its defined terms and conditions precedent spelled out.

**Field assignment.** Bring back a **real contract clause or a real work handoff** from your job — a SOW scope section, a vendor agreement paragraph, an onboarding handoff doc, a ticket that crossed teams. Audit it for its type declarations (defined terms), its preconditions (conditions precedent), and its silences. Redact as needed. Journal entry.

### Session 7 — Bias, defaults, and "correct for whom"

**In-class focus.** This is [`../index.md`](../index.md) §7 — ethics, bias, and specification — lived. The framework's distinctive claim: **bias in an AI system is, in substantial part, an underspecification problem.** When an AI's output reflects a default the user didn't choose — a default audience, a default tone, a default notion of "professional," a default demographic frame, a default of whose perspective counts as neutral — that is an **underspecified objective**: the criteria for "correct" output didn't say whose context it had to be correct *in*. So the operative questions, applied to participants' real tools and workflows: *what was specified, what was assumed, and whose perspective was treated as the default?* We work this on real material — the résumé screener that has a default idea of a good candidate, the marketing copy generator that has a default customer, the summarizer that decides on its own what's "important," the translation tool whose defaults are someone's locale and not someone else's. The course's standing evaluation question, from [`../index.md`](../index.md) §7, gets its full workout here: when you check an AI output, you ask not only "is this correct?" but **"correct for whom, under what assumptions, and with what consequences?"** This is not a separate ethics module bolted on; it's the Assumption Audit's *context* question and the Verification Principle's *identify-limits* behavior, turned toward the question of whose defaults got encoded.

**Field assignment.** Pick one AI tool or automated workflow you actually rely on, and identify a default it's making *on your behalf* that you never chose — an audience, a tone, a notion of "important," a demographic frame. Write down what choosing it deliberately would look like, and whether you can. Journal entry.

### Session 8 — Capstone share-out

**In-class focus.** Each participant presents one **before/after** from their own work: (1) an artifact from their job; (2) the underspecification they found in it — which of the four audit questions it failed; (3) the **revised spec**, with explicit acceptance criteria; (4) the **verification** they ran on the revised result — what they checked and how, and whether the domain let them prove or only spot-check; (5) **what changed** — fewer revision cycles, fewer misfires, a thing that actually met the need. Then **peer verification** of each other's specs ([`../index.md`](../index.md) §12.3): partners trade revised specs and ask the §12.3 question — *can I reproduce the result, or predict it, from this spec alone, with no conversation with the author?* Not "is this a good spec?" but "did it work as a spec?" Where it breaks is where it's still incomplete, and the author leaves with a note on what to tighten. We close with a debrief: **what transfers to the rest of your work** — which of these four behaviors you'll keep doing on Monday, and where.

**Field assignment.** None after Session 8 — the capstone *is* the final field assignment, prepared in the week before. The verification journal is submitted complete.

## 4. Assessment

**Pass/fail. No grades.** This is a not-for-credit continuing-education class, not a course carrying a letter grade, and the framework's stance on assessment ([`../index.md`](../index.md) §10.3) is the right one for adults under stakes: the point is to demonstrate the four Verification-Principle behaviors, not to be ranked.

The single instrument is the **verification journal** — the same instrument as the 8th-grade anchor course ([`../index.md`](../index.md) §10.3, where "AI generates all code; students specify, trace, verify, and explain" and "assessment is pass/fail based on verification journals"). For adults the journal documents, for each field assignment: the **spec** you wrote, the **generation** you got back (from an AI or a human delegate), any **trace** you ran (mandatory for the code-thread entries; spot-check notes for the document entries), the **gap** you found, and the **revision** you made — plus the **capstone** before/after at the end.

**Per-entry template** (one short page per field assignment):

| Field | What goes here |
|---|---|
| **What I asked for** | The actual prompt / brief / ticket / clause / delegation, verbatim (redacted as needed). |
| **What I expected** | What you thought a correct result would be — written *before* you looked at what you got. |
| **What I got** | The actual result, or a faithful summary of it. |
| **The gap** | Where did the interpreter decide something you left undecided? Which of the four audit questions — types / boundaries / defaults / context — does this trace back to? |
| **The revised spec** | The rewritten artifact, with explicit acceptance criteria. |
| **How I checked** | What you did to verify the revised result. For a code-thread entry: the trace, and the argument for why it's correct (or the bug). For a document entry: what you spot-checked, what you couldn't, and whether the domain even permitted a proof. |

**Pass condition.** A *complete* journal — an entry for each field assignment, in the template above — that, taken together, **shows all four Verification-Principle behaviors**: at least one entry where the participant *specified* before generating; at least one where they *evaluated* a result against the spec rather than against "seems fine"; at least one where they *explained* why a correct result is correct (the code-thread trace is the natural home for this, but a document entry can carry it too); and at least one where they *identified the limits* — preconditions, un-handled cases, who the result is and isn't correct for. Plus the **capstone share-out** delivered in Session 8: a real before/after with the spec revision and the verification on the record. That's the bar. There is nothing else to pass.

## 5. Prerequisites

**None — beyond "you use, or expect to use, AI tools in your work."** That's the whole prerequisite. If you write prompts, briefs, tickets, PRDs, RFPs, SOWs, contract clauses, or delegation emails — or you direct people or tools who act on them — this course is for you, whatever your field.

**The code-trace thread is explicitly *not* a coding prerequisite.** Per [`../index.md`](../index.md) §6.1, participants *read and trace* code; they never write it. The thread is read-only and fully scaffolded — a short AI-written program, handed out with the spec it was built from, walked through line by line in the room. Following the logic is the ask; producing syntactically correct programs is not — that's the AI's job here, exactly as arithmetic is the calculator's job in a statistics course. If you've never written a line of code, you can do this thread, and the course is built so that you get one solid taste of provable verification out of it.

**Bring real work artifacts** — prompts, briefs, tickets, clauses, handoffs, delegation emails that you actually use — **with anything confidential redacted.** The course only works on real material; "redacted but real" beats "made up but tidy" every time. (See §7 on what not to bring at all.)

## 6. AI access

**Model-agnostic** ([`../index.md`](../index.md) §6.2). The course does not require a particular AI tool, and it does not teach one. In practice, participants will mostly bring **their employer's sanctioned tool** — whatever copilot, assistant, or model their organization has approved — and that's the right thing for them to be practicing on, because it's the thing they'll actually use on Monday. The course specifies the **interaction**, not the tool: every exercise is phrased as "prompt the AI to draft X meeting these criteria, then verify it," never as "in [Product], click [Menu]." If a participant has no employer tool, a browser-based assistant with a free tier is sufficient for everything the course does. The skill being built — specify, evaluate, explain, identify limits — is the same regardless of which model is on the other end, and it stays valid as the models change.

**A note that has no counterpart in the 8th-grade course** (where the AI access is school-provisioned and the data is classroom exercises): **do not paste confidential, proprietary, or regulated data into consumer AI tools, and check your employer's AI-use policy before you bring anything to a session.** Client data, personnel data, unreleased financials, anything under NDA, anything covered by HIPAA / FERPA / GLBA / attorney-client privilege — none of it goes into a consumer chatbot, and a lot of it doesn't belong in a classroom either. The course's standing instruction is **"bring the real thing, redacted"**: keep the structure and the language that made the artifact underspecified, strip the identifying and confidential content. When in doubt, leave it out and bring a different artifact.
